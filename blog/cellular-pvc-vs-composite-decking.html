PVC Decking vs Composite Decking: The Full Case for PVC

Both look like premium decking in the showroom. Only one of them still has wood inside it. Here is the complete, evidence-based comparison, including a straight answer on the one question every skeptical homeowner in a cold climate eventually asks.

Published September 14, 2026

Every decking sales conversation eventually reaches the same fork. On one side is composite decking, wood fiber blended with recycled plastic and wrapped in a protective cap. On the other is cellular PVC, a board that is 100 percent plastic with no wood fiber anywhere in it. Both are marketed as low-maintenance wood alternatives. Both look convincing under showroom lighting. But only one of them can genuinely say there is nothing inside the board for water, mold, or rot to find. This is the complete case for PVC, built from the material science, not the marketing copy, plus an honest answer to the cold-climate objection that comes up more than any other.

What each board actually is, underneath the finish

Composite decking is a wood-plastic composite. Manufacturers blend recycled polyethylene or polypropylene with wood flour or wood fiber, typically sawdust and sawmill byproduct, then extrude that blend into a board and apply a polymer cap over the surface to hold color and block moisture. The wood content in that core commonly runs from roughly a third to more than half of the board by weight, depending on the manufacturer.

Cellular PVC decking, the category AmeriDex builds on, is manufactured entirely from polyvinyl chloride in a closed-cell foamed structure, with a weatherable ASA cap bonded to the surface. There is no wood flour, no wood fiber, and no cellulosic filler of any kind in the core. It is plastic from the surface to the center of the board, on every face and at every cut end.

That single difference in composition is the reason the two materials age so differently once they leave the showroom and spend a decade outdoors.

The core problem with composite: it still has to protect wood from water

Wood fiber is hydrophilic. It absorbs water, and once water reaches it, the fiber swells, and swelling is the mechanism behind cupping, soft spots, and the internal mold growth that has driven multiple class-action claims against major composite manufacturers over mold and moisture failures inside the cap.

The cap is the only thing standing between that wood fiber and the weather, and the cap is not permanent protection. It is a surface treatment, and surfaces get breached in ordinary use:

  • Every cut end. Cutting a board to length or notching it around a post exposes raw wood fiber at that cut, and many installers do not field-seal every cut the way the fine print recommends.
  • Every scratch, drag mark, and fastener hole. Furniture, grills, dropped tools, and screws all nick the cap. Each nick is a new entry point for moisture into the wood underneath.
  • Three-sided caps. Some composite lines cap only the top and two edges, leaving the bottom of the board uncapped and dependent on airflow, which is the least reliable protection on a low, shaded, or poorly ventilated deck.

Independent materials research on wood-polymer composites confirms the underlying mechanism: even capped systems absorb measurable moisture over repeated wet-dry cycles, and that absorption is directly tied to the wood-fiber content of the core, not the cap alone.

Cellular PVC never has this problem, for the simplest reason possible: there is no wood fiber anywhere in the board for water to reach. A cut end is just more PVC. A scratch is cosmetic. There is nothing inside the board that can swell, rot, or feed mold, because there is nothing organic inside the board at all.

Composite spends its whole service life protecting the wood fiber inside it. Cellular PVC has nothing inside it left to protect.

Now, the honest answer on thermal movement, because a good customer already asked

Here is a straight answer to a question that deserves one: PVC decking does expand and contract more than composite decking as temperatures swing. That is a real, measurable, published property, and any comparison that ignores it is not a serious comparison.

Cellular PVC has a coefficient of linear thermal expansion of roughly 3.0 to 3.5 × 10-5 inches per inch per degree Fahrenheit, meaningfully higher than capped composite, which typically runs closer to 1.8 to 2.2 × 10-5. In practical terms, a 20-foot PVC board can move roughly a third of an inch in length across a 50-degree temperature swing, almost entirely along its length rather than its width or thickness. In a climate like Indiana's, where temperatures cross the freezing line dozens of times in a single winter, that is a fair thing for a homeowner to ask about before they sign a contract.

The reason this is not a defect is the same reason vinyl siding, vinyl fencing, and PVC trim have been standard products in cold-climate states for decades: thermal movement in PVC is not a mystery, it is a known, published, engineered variable, and it is fully designed around at the installation stage, not discovered as a problem afterward.

How the movement is actually controlled

  • Gap sizing is temperature-based, not guesswork. Installers set side gaps around 1/8 to 3/16 inch and set end gaps according to the temperature on installation day, tighter in cold weather, wider in hot weather, using published manufacturer tables. This is a standard trade practice, the same discipline used for vinyl fencing and PVC trim in freeze-thaw states.
  • AmeriDex boards are face screwed to every joist at 16 inches on center, with two screws per joist. That is a materially different fastening approach than a board held only at its two ends. Two screws at every joist, spaced 16 inches apart down the full run, means the board is anchored along its entire length at dozens of points, not just two. Any thermal movement gets distributed across all of those closely spaced fastening points instead of concentrating as one large shift at a single free end. That density of fastening is exactly what keeps the board flat and controls movement in small, predictable increments rather than large ones, which is also why AmeriDex holds up well underfoot with none of the give some end-fastened or clip-only systems develop over time.
  • Face screwing is also simpler on cellular PVC. Most composite manufacturers require predrilling a pilot hole for every face screw, especially near board ends and in cold-weather installation, to keep the denser wood-fiber core from cracking or splitting around the fastener. Cellular PVC does not carry that same restriction, so the installer can drive two screws per joist at 16 inches on center without pre-drilling every single hole first, which keeps the installation both faster and less prone to a missed pilot hole splitting a board months later.
  • The movement is dimensional, not structural. A board that grows or shrinks a fraction of an inch across a season is not weakening, cracking, or losing load capacity. It is simply occupying the gap it was installed with. Compare that to what happens when water gets inside a composite board's wood-fiber core and freezes: the trapped water expands and can split the material from the inside, which is structural damage, not a dimensional adjustment.

The AmeriDex Dryspace System is engineered around exactly this reality. The Dexerdry seal, an automotive-grade TPE component, locks onto each board at installation and is designed to flex with normal seasonal board movement while continuing to shed water across the deck surface. Combined with 16 inch on-center face screwing into every joist, the board is both fully secured and free to move in the small, evenly distributed increments the gap spacing was designed for. The system was not designed assuming a static, motionless board. It was designed for a board that moves predictably within an engineered gap, held firmly the entire way down its length, which is precisely what cellular PVC does.

Two rows deserve a plain-language note. On thermal movement, PVC genuinely moves more, and that is why gap spacing at installation is not optional, it is the entire control mechanism, the same as it is for vinyl fencing or siding. On freeze-thaw, the more important fact is that composite's risk comes from trapped moisture, a chemistry problem, while PVC's movement is a dimensional installation variable with a known, published fix. Those are not the same category of risk, even though both involve winter temperatures.

Why the core material matters even more in a sealed dry-deck system

Choosing a deck board is one decision. Building a system that keeps the space underneath the deck dry is another, and it raises the stakes on which core material carries that responsibility. The AmeriDex Dryspace System locks every cellular PVC board onto a Dexerdry seal as the deck is built, closing the gap between boards so rain sheets across the surface and runs off the edge instead of dripping through onto the framing below.

In that system, the deck boards are not just a walking surface, they are the structural component of a waterproofing system. That is not a job for a board that can absorb moisture at any cut, scratch, or breached cap. A wood-free cellular PVC board is the right foundation for that seal specifically because it is dimensionally predictable and indifferent to water on every face, at every cut, in every season.

For the full mechanics of how the boards and seal work together, the above-joist deck drainage guide covers the system in detail, and why decks rot from the top down explains the moisture cycle that makes core material the decision that matters most.

The bottom line

Composite is a genuine upgrade over untreated wood, and a well-built composite deck can perform for decades. But it still carries wood fiber inside a cap, and caps get cut, scratched, and eventually breached, which is the failure mode that shows up in the moisture and mold complaints filed against major composite brands. Cellular PVC removes wood from the equation entirely. It does move more with temperature, that part is true and worth saying plainly, but that movement is a known, engineered, installation-stage variable, not an open-ended risk, and it does not carry the moisture-driven failure mode that composite does. That is why PVC wins on moisture, cut-edge durability, and long-term reliability, and it is why AmeriDex builds the Dryspace System around it.
